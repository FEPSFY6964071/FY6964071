Introduction

          Over the past decade, the percentage of greenhouse gas emissions, primarily carbon dioxide (CO2) has increased by 0.32% per year (ECIU, 2025), which is better than pre-Paris, but is still at an incline. Internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles play a part as to why this value is increasing (Negri, M & Bieker, G, 2025), degrading urban air quality (UCS, 2025). This decline poses severe public health risks, affecting respiratory and cardiovascular systems, causing people to contract various illnesses (Enyenihi, A, 2024, para 5). Furthermore, this environmental toil extends to surrounding ecosystems and living organisms that require clean air to thrive (Enyenihi, A, 2024, para 4). First, how ICE vehicles have damaged the environment over the past decade and the causes of it, followed by how we can reduce the damage and improve for the future.

Damage & Causes

     Internal combustion engine vehicles have been a mass contributor to air pollution for decades, by emitting pollutants into the atmosphere through burning fossil fuels (EPA, 2026), with most of these greenhouse gas emissions being a result from the combustion of petroleum-based products in ICE vehicles (EPA, 2026). These emissions include gases, such as carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O). Over time, these pollutants accumulate, especially in traffic dense areas such as cities and motorways, reducing the overall air quality in those areas. To fuel internal combustion engines, it takes mining and separating crude oil into different sections to make diesel fuel (Britannica, 2026), which itself releases toxic gases to the atmosphere. Combined with the burning of fuels inside these engines, it leads to a massive output of gases into the air only from the transportation sector.
Transportation remains as one of the highest leading causes of greenhouse gas emissions in developed countries. One example being the United States of America, with the emissions from fossil fuel combustion increasing by “19%” between 1990 to 2022 (EPA, 2026). Due to the increase of CO2 emissions, it does mean that pollution of the air has increased as well, leading to many environments being affected along with the organisms that live in it, ranging from humans to other animals. For example, due to this increase of emissions, it has impacted human health in ways that lead to death, such as undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress (WHO, 2023). Not only that, but it also creates harmful environments for people to live in, varying from heat waves, storms, floods, and hurricanes destroying people’s homes. As well as destroying homes, it destroys farmland with many farmers losing their yield, not being able to support themselves, and food not being able to be delivered to supermarkets, as all the agriculture has died out leading to shortages of food. This then affects peoples’ basic needs as they do not have access to food regularly. 
Additionally, it leaves a permanent impact on animals that thrive off this land that has been damaged. Not only do they lose their source of food, but they also lose their habitats due to the constant changing of climates, overall leading to many species becoming extinct.

Solutions

     As the leading cause to all this harm and damage is because of internal combustion engine vehicles, one of the solutions that people can take is switching from ICE to electric vehicles (EV’s). This is a great solution and has already shown impact over the past couple years. It reduces the harm caused to the environment, not releasing toxic pollutants into the atmosphere, and consistently show that electric vehicles produce a significantly lower life cycle of CO2 emissions, compared to internal combustion engines (U.S. Department of Energy, 2024). Furthermore, comparing EV’s to ICE vehicles, electricity is a reproducible energy source meaning that it is in constant production, whereas fossil fuels are non-renewable, meaning that they can be exhausted and are not suitable forever.
One solution that the UK government is implementing is to try and phase out sales of new petrol and diesel cars from 2030, supporting the zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) transition. As stated by Anthony Browne (2024), the goal is that vehicles manufacturers will produce up to 80% of new cars and 70% of new vans sold in the UK will be zero emission, increasing to 100% by 2035. This would overall decrease the amount of emissions caused by transport vehicles, as due them stopping the production, there won’t be any more vehicles to cause that damage.
Another solution is to switch to hydrogen cars. These are cars which run on pure hydrogen (H2) and work similarly to how battery-electric cars work. This is by having the hydrogen pass through a membrane with oxygen (O2) from the air, which produces electricity (which turns the wheels through a motor) and water vapour as its emissions (Voelcker J, 2024, para. 6). It seems like a sufficient solution, however there are still disadvantages such as the availability of hydrogen fuel being scarce with it being a relatively new product, as well as with the actual cost for the fuel being more expensive than ICE vehicles for covering the same distance (Voelcker J, 2024, para. 21).

Conclusion

     This paper examined the current situation with the vehicles used everyday by a majority of the world. Currently most of the world is using internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles as a source of transport, with the option of switching to electric vehicles or hydrogen cell vehicles. Although one is more reliable for everyday transport and use, others are more useful for trying to cut down global greenhouse gas emissions, trying to make the world a cleaner place and revert the damage that has been caused by ICE vehicles, despite the problems they may face along the way.
